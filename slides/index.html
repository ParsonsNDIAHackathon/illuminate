Thirty seconds of framing: this is one system that answers both use cases, because both are the same question asked from different roots.

The problem

A program office can name its primes.

It cannot name what its primes depend on.

  • Tier 1 is contract data. Tier 2 is a footnote. Tier 3 is a phone call.
  • Risk rarely sits at tier 1. It arrives from three hops away — an ownership chain through two intermediaries, a shared director, a sole-source casting house. Screen the vendor and you find nothing. The exposure is in the relationship, not the record.
  • The useful question is never “is this vendor bad?”
    It is “what reaches my program, and by what route?”
The pitch in one line: document review is per-vendor, and the risk is between vendors.

What it does

Model the dependency graph. Attach evidence to every edge. Ask it questions.

Illuminate does not know it is about defense. Point it at a DoD program and it answers one use case; point it at the government as a buying organisation and it answers the other. Same graph, same scorer, different root.

UC-7 · root = a program

Supply chain illumination

Walk suppliers tier by tier. Surface foreign control, sole-source chokepoints and shared people — then show what flows back up to the program.

UC-11 · root = the buyer

Vendor risk assessment

One vendor report: identity, geography, control, people, affiliations and a scored risk profile with citations — carried on every organisation and person, not just the one on screen.

Emphasise: the risk score is a property of every node in the graph, computed in one bulk pass, so the canvas, the lists, the report and Cypher all read one number.

  • Every answer is a subgraph. The executed query is always shown, so a reader can check the work.
  • Nothing is asserted without a record. Facts arrive as claims, each pointing at the artifact that evidences it. Authoritative sources commit; open-source facts stay staged until a human or a second independent source agrees.

  • LittleSis — officers, directors and their other seats
  • SEC EDGAR — tickers, CIKs, filings
  • SAM.gov Entity — registration, CAGE, NAICS

  • UN Consolidated List — the multilateral counterpart
  • SAM.gov Exclusions — 33,861 debarment records, screened locally

The seed builds the program from live sources and caches every response as a fixture — 253 cached calls — so the graph rebuilds offline in about a minute. Jurisdiction, conflict and hazard tables are a committed snapshot with a stated date, for the same reason.

If asked why fixtures: a demo that silently depended on a live third-party index would be unreproducible, and a score you cannot reproduce is not a score.

The two routes in

Neither one is written down anywhere.

Thread 1 · ownership

Sole-source castings, three hops from a state group

A tier-3 sole-source supplier of investment castings, incorporated in Delaware and manufacturing in China → owned 100% by a Hong Kong holding company → owned 100% by a state-owned provincial metals group.

Nothing in the data says “ultimate parent.” That is what walking the ownership chain to its root discovers. Country is four different edges — incorporated, operates, manufactures, parent-seated — so a Delaware shell that manufactures abroad is representable without a lie.

Thread 2 · people

Supplier → employee → designated group

The vendor is clean. The employee is clean. The tie is a role edge to a designated cyber-threat group with an OFAC and a UN listing against it.

Reachable only by walking the association. A former director of the host company now sits on the castings supplier's board too — a revolving-door lead that needs no second supplier in the graph.

Both threads are simulated, as the brief allows. Every synthetic node, edge and claim carries simulated: true and the UI discloses it — 7 nodes, 14 edges.

The point of the whole thing

Colour the graph by risk, and watch it flow.

Same canvas, one encoding change. The hot nodes sit at the edge of the network, and the colour walks inward along the edges that carry the exposure — three hops, then two, then the prime, then the program.

Say the sentence: risk is not a property of a vendor, it is a property of a path. Proximity is the dimension only a graph can compute, and it is the one that catches a vendor whose own record is spotless.

Why it flows

Risk is a property of the node — computed over the whole graph.

DimensionWeightWhat it reads
Designation×3.0 The node's own screens: OFAC, UN, SAM exclusions, DoD §1260H. A hit here is the finding.
Proximity×2.0 Degrees of separation from a designated party, walked up to three hops over ownership, personnel and commercial edges. The dimension only a graph can compute. Asymmetric on supply edges — buying from a designated party puts its problem inside your product.
Dependency×2.0 What a consumer inherits from suppliers it cannot replace. Sole sources are graded on the worst, never summed — a long list of unremarkable ones must not manufacture a severe finding.
Foreign control×1.5 Incorporation, ultimate-parent seat, operations, manufacture — read separately, worst wins.
Financial distress×1.5 Filing behaviour: lapsed SAM registration, late-filing notices, a listed company that stopped filing.
Regional exposure×1.0 Conflict and natural hazard where the entity physically operates — blind to incorporation, since a hurricane does not read the paperwork.
Adverse media×0.75The GDELT screen, where one has run.

A signal that returned no data is not a zero. A dimension with nothing to go on abstains, so every node also carries risk_confidence — the share of the model's weight that answered — drawn next to the score everywhere. And nothing is scored that cannot be shown: every dimension carries the ids of the nodes and edges it was computed from. Click one, and the route lights up on the canvas.

Sole-source status is scored on the customer, not the supplier — being a sole source says nothing about whether that company will fail. The exposure belongs to whoever depends on it.

The chat interface

Ask it in plain language. Get a subgraph and the query that produced it.

› show sole-source suppliers
› which suppliers have a foreign ultimate parent?
› which vendors are within two hops of a designated party?

The model picks between named templates and generated Cypher. Generated statements are classified and validated — label and relationship allowlist, hops capped at six, LIMIT clamped, CALL db.* and LOAD CSV rejected — and the executed query is always shown.

Without a model key, typed questions match to templates by intent. The app degrades; it does not fail.

Text-to-Cypher fails in ways that look like answers. That is why templates are the default and the query is on screen every time.

The chat interface

Encoding is data, too.

› for all of the program's vendors, highlight goods in purple and services in yellow

That becomes style operations on element ids. Fill and stroke are palette names, never hex, resolved to theme-tested tokens at the client; an unknown name falls back to neutral with a warning.

The legend is derived from the operations rather than written by hand — so the key on screen cannot drift from what was actually drawn.

The chat interface · generate a report

A report is a node too.

  • Writes a Report node holding the finished document, joined to its subject by REPORTS_ON and to everything it names by CITES.
  • It draws on its own canvas layer, and every finding traces back to the nodes it was computed from.
  • Regenerating rewrites that same node from current data — no drift of near-identical documents.
  • Honest no data rows where a source was not available, and the score is stated with how much of the model stood behind it.

Narrative families — interlocks, government ties, political exposure — appear separately as context. They explain a score; they do not move it.

The chat interface · writes

It asks before it writes.

› add Acme Castings as a tier-3 supplier to AVIAN, LLC

A write executes inside a transaction that is rolled back to obtain real counters, then held for you: approve once, approve this shape for the session, edit, or refuse.

Destructive statements require the affected count to be acknowledged and never auto-approve. A refusal goes back to the model as a tool result.

The same gate sits in front of external agents over MCP — one tool contract, two transports, one dispatch. Claude Desktop or Claude Code can drive it, under the same rules.

D1 and D4 together. The impact count is real because the transaction really ran — that is the detail people ask about.

Candour

Honest limits.

  • Entity resolution is the whole game. UEI, CAGE and LEI carry it; the residue is name matching, tuned to refuse the subset trap, and every fuzzy merge records its method and confidence.
  • Sub-award coverage is uneven, so tier depth varies by program. People are harder to resolve than companies, and coverage skews to large listed firms.
  • The risk score is a triage instrument, not an adjudication. Financial distress is read from filing behaviour, never from the statements themselves — and a private sub-tier vendor with no filings produces no signal at all, which is the common case.
  • A clear result is not a clearance. The §1260H roster is an excerpt of the published annex, and every result says so — including the clear ones.
  • SAM.gov's personal-key tier allows about ten Entity calls a day, so registration details fill in slowly. The exclusion screen does not depend on it — it runs against the public daily extract.
Do not skip this slide. Judges trust a team that names its own failure modes more than one that does not have any.

What's next

Cut for the three days — and where it goes.

Cut on purpose, scoped and understood
  • A vector index over filings, so “who else mentions this supplier?” is answerable from 10-K and 10-Q text rather than structured fields alone.
  • Multi-tenant auth. Today: one workspace, one set of encrypted keys.
  • Live feeds behind the conflict and hazard tables — a committed snapshot with a stated date stands in, deliberately.
  • Full-text financial-statement analysis, beyond filing behaviour.
  • Market data — wired, keyed off.
Where it goes next
  • Monitoring, not snapshots. Re-run the graph on a schedule and alert on the delta — a new designation, a lapsed registration, an ownership change. Claims are already timestamped and evidenced, so diffing them is the natural next step.
  • Deeper than tier 2. Sub-award reporting runs out; customs and bill-of-lading data, plus supplier-submitted BOMs, would carry the walk further down.
  • Portfolio view. Run one risk pass across many programs and rank them — the scorer is already graph-wide, so this is mostly a question of UI.
  • What-if. “If this supplier fails, what stops?” The dependency dimension computes it; it just needs to be exposed as a simulation.
  • Into the acquisition workflow. MCP already exposes the tools, so a contracting officer's own agent could call this pre-award. Air-gapped is the same stack with a local model.
  • Entity resolution as a service, with human-in-the-loop merge review.
The honest framing: nothing in the left column was hard to see coming, we just chose the graph and the evidence model first. The right column is what the architecture was built to allow.
